Why DOMS peaks around 48 hours

After unfamiliar exercise, soreness builds over the next one to three days, even though the damage is tiny and already falling. The soreness follows the immune cells, not the damage. Drag through the hours to see what they are doing.

What this means for you

  • Soreness at 48 hours is a sign your muscles are adapting, not a sign of injury. Sore is not harm.
  • Gentle movement eases DOMS for a while. Go easier on the same muscles until it settles, then build up again.
  • Do the same session again and you will usually be much less sore. This is the repeated bout effect.
  • Scientists are still working out exactly what causes DOMS. This shows the leading explanation: immune cells and the muscle release messengers that make the sensors touchier while repair happens.

See a doctor if the pain is severe and still getting worse after three days, the area is very swollen, or your urine turns dark brown after very hard exercise.

Tendons: the 24-hour check

Tendons respond to load more slowly than muscle. After a heavy or springy session, collagen breakdown rises first and building catches up over the next few days. A sensitive tendon often feels fine during activity, then tells you the next morning whether the dose was right. This is different from muscle DOMS: next-day tendon pain is feedback about the dose, not a sign of adaptation.

What this means for you

  • Judge today’s session by how the tendon feels tomorrow morning, not during. Tendon pain often eases as you warm up.
  • Pain up to 5/10 during and after can be fine, as long as it has settled by the next morning and morning pain and stiffness are not creeping up week to week.
  • If it is worse tomorrow, the dose was too much. Ease off the next session, then build again.
  • Don’t stop loading altogether: tendons need load to get stronger. Isometric holds can settle pain.
  • Give heavy or springy sessions time for building to catch up, often 48–72 hours.
  • Tendon pain doesn’t match damage either. Scans can look worse than a tendon feels, or better.

      Look back at the start

      Does pain mean something is damaged?
      What pain science says

      Sometimes. Pain and damage are related but not the same. You can have damage without pain, and pain without damage. Pain is a protector: a prediction of what could happen, not a report of what has happened.

      When is pain helpful?
      What pain science says

      When it protects tissue that is healing, like a fresh ankle sprain. Pain changes your behaviour so you give the area extra care while it repairs.

      When is pain unhelpful?
      What pain science says

      When it protects you after you no longer need protecting, such as after tissue has healed. Pain can be learned, and it can be relearned too.
